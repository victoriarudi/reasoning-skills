# 2x2 Matrix Skill

This skill is for building a 2x2 matrix that helps make complexity legible.

A 2x2 matrix is not primarily a drawing exercise. The matrix itself is trivial. The real work is choosing the axes.

Use this skill when the user wants Claude to:
- compare options
- make a decision
- prioritize actions
- diagnose a terrain
- map competitors
- understand a market or category
- identify portfolio balance
- surface hidden tradeoffs
- clarify why a decision is stuck
- compress a complex reality into two dimensions without collapsing it into mush

The output should not present the matrix as "the answer." The matrix is a thinking tool. It should make the reality more legible, reveal tradeoffs, and provoke better judgment.

## Core principle

The axes are the argument.
The quadrants are the consequences.

A good 2x2 does not merely organize information. It makes a claim about what actually matters.

## Core cognitive functions

This skill operates through two distinct cognitive functions. These are not personalities. They are irreducible lenses.

### 1. Axis Creator

The Axis Creator asks:
- What are the dimensions that actually structure this decision or terrain?
- Which axes are independent rather than correlated?
- Which pair creates the most meaningful spread?
- Which dimensions are specific enough to be useful?
- What hidden variables are being compressed into each axis?
- What worldview does this pair encode?

Its job is to build clean, decision-relevant, terrain-revealing axes.

### 2. Differentiation Challenger

The Differentiation Challenger asks:
- Is this just the first obvious matrix?
- Do these axes actually differentiate the items, or flatten them?
- What less-obvious axis pair would reveal a different truth?
- What is hidden by the first matrix?
- Does this frame create useful tension, or only polite structure?
- Are these two final matrices truly different?

Its job is to resist cliché axes, weak differentiation, false clarity, and redundant framing.

Claude must use both functions every time.

## Mandatory operating logic

Every time this skill is used, Claude must follow this sequence.

### Step 1: Name the object

First, define what is being mapped.

Possible objects include:
- decision options
- strategic directions
- products
- markets
- risks
- competitors
- features
- portfolio elements
- internal tensions
- categories
- themes
- initiatives
- business units
- problem types

State the object clearly before designing the matrix.

### Step 2: Name the mapping purpose

Then identify the actual purpose of the matrix.

Possible purposes include:
- choosing among options
- prioritizing actions
- diagnosing a terrain
- comparing competitors
- identifying portfolio balance
- surfacing tradeoffs
- clarifying why a decision is stuck
- revealing structural patterns
- locating white space or gaps
- understanding clustering or asymmetry

The right axes depend on purpose. Do not skip this step.

### Step 3: Clarifying questions from both functions

Before generating candidate axes, both cognitive functions must ask only the clarifying questions necessary to avoid weak, premature, or obvious framing.

If the object and purpose are already sufficiently clear, each function should explicitly say:
- **No additional clarifying questions**

Do not skip this step.

#### Axis Creator in the clarifying phase
Its job here is to sharpen the object, the purpose, and the possible dimensions of the matrix.

It asks questions such as:
- What exactly is the object under review?
- Are we comparing options, mapping a terrain, or diagnosing why something is stuck?
- What are the items the matrix needs to distinguish between?
- What dimensions are already present in the reality?
- What appears to matter most in this case?
- What criteria or constraints cannot be ignored?
- What would make the matrix structurally useful rather than merely neat?

#### Differentiation Challenger in the clarifying phase
Its job here is to resist cliché framing, surface hidden assumptions, and pressure-test usefulness before the matrix is built.

It asks questions such as:
- What is the first obvious way to frame this, and why might it be too easy?
- What kind of spread or differentiation would actually help here?
- What hidden assumptions may be shaping the problem already?
- What would make this matrix genuinely useful instead of decorative?
- What would be misleading to compress too quickly?
- Is the goal to decide, to see, or to challenge the current framing itself?
- What would an executive wrongly conclude if the axes are too obvious?

### Step 4: Only then generate candidate axes

Generate at least 4 candidate axis pairs before settling on the final matrix options.

These candidate pairs may be shown explicitly, but the skill must force axis exploration before choosing the final two.

Do not fixate on the first obvious axis pair.

### Step 5: Test axis quality and reject weak pairs

Before selecting the final matrices, evaluate candidate axis pairs against the following criteria.

#### Strong axes

Strong axes are:
- **independent**: movement on one axis does not automatically imply movement on the other
- **specific**: precise enough that placement is not vague or arbitrary
- **relevant**: they matter for the real object and purpose
- **differentiating**: they spread the items meaningfully or reveal useful clustering
- **interpretively rich**: they reveal something real, not just something tidy
- **legible**: a decision-maker can understand why these dimensions matter

#### Weak axes

Weak axes are:
- **vague**: "good vs bad," "better vs worse," "strategic value" without precision
- **redundant**: both axes measure the same underlying thing
- **obvious but unhelpful**: they sound reasonable but do not reveal anything new
- **non-differentiating**: everything lands in the same quadrant or along one diagonal
- **irrelevant**: they miss what actually matters
- **overloaded**: one axis hides too many dimensions at once
- **decorative**: they produce a neat matrix without improving understanding

Claude must explicitly challenge candidate axes by asking:
- Is this just the first obvious matrix?
- Do these axes actually differentiate the items?
- Are the axes independent?
- Does this pair reveal something useful, or does it merely sound reasonable?
- What important dimension is being left off the grid?
- What worldview does this axis pair encode?

### Step 6: Produce two final 2x2 matrix options

Produce **two materially different matrix options** for the same object.

They must not be slight rephrasings of the same underlying logic.

Claude must decide which two cuts are most useful for the object and purpose at hand.

The two matrices should reflect genuinely different ways of structuring the same reality.

For example:
- one matrix may be better for decision-making, another for terrain diagnosis
- one may reveal portfolio logic, another execution risk
- one may reveal user value, another strategic leverage
- one may reveal market structure, another organizational feasibility

Do not default to generic pairs like value vs effort unless they are genuinely the strongest choice.

### Step 7: Define the four quadrants for each matrix

For each final matrix, Claude must explicitly define the four quadrants using the actual poles of the axes.

Do not assign generic quadrant meanings in advance.
The quadrants derive their meaning from the chosen axes.

For each quadrant, explain:
- what this quadrant represents
- what kinds of items belong there
- what this quadrant tends to mean strategically or diagnostically
- what opportunity it creates
- what risk, tradeoff, or caution it carries

Use the actual axis poles when naming the quadrants.

Examples:
- if the axes are high vs low, use high/low labels
- if the axes are existing vs new, use existing/new labels
- if the axes are predictable vs unpredictable, bounded vs unbounded, use those exact terms

The goal is not only to place items, but to interpret the space itself.

### Step 8: Compare and recommend

After presenting Matrix Option A and Matrix Option B:
- compare them directly
- explain what each one reveals that the other hides
- explain which matrix is stronger for the stated purpose
- explain when the alternative matrix would still be useful
- surface any cautions, blind spots, or oversimplifications

The final recommendation matters. Do not stop at presentation.

## Decision mode vs terrain mode

A 2x2 matrix can be used in two main ways.

### Decision mode

Use this mode when the matrix is meant to help choose, prioritize, sequence, or reduce uncertainty among options.

Examples:
- which market to enter
- which feature to prioritize
- which initiative to fund
- which strategic path to pursue
- what to do first

In decision mode, emphasize:
- the real decision being made
- the two dimensions that drive the decision
- where each option sits
- what tradeoffs become visible
- which matrix is most decision-useful
- what move follows

### Terrain mode

Use this mode when the matrix is meant to make a landscape more legible.

Examples:
- mapping competitors
- mapping AI product categories
- mapping executive problem types
- mapping customer segments
- mapping white space
- mapping clusters and asymmetries

In terrain mode, emphasize:
- why these axes reveal the terrain
- where the clusters are
- where the gaps are
- what patterns and asymmetries emerge
- what assumptions the field appears to encode
- what the map suggests but does not prove

Claude must identify which mode is primary before building the matrix.

## Rules

- Do not rush to the first obvious axis pair.
- Do not produce vague or generic axes.
- Do not present two near-identical matrices as if they are different.
- Do not collapse multiple unrelated dimensions into one overloaded axis.
- Do not treat the matrix as a decision machine.
- Do not pretend the quadrants are objectively true if placement is judgment-based.
- Do not hide the limitations of the frame.
- Do not force a matrix if the object is too incoherent or underdefined; in that case, say what is missing.

## Limitations to acknowledge

A 2x2 matrix always compresses reality. That is its strength and its limitation.

Claude should explicitly acknowledge, when relevant:
- reality has more than two dimensions
- placement is often judgment-based rather than exact
- boundaries between high and low are not neutral
- the matrix is usually a snapshot, not a trajectory
- a third variable may matter more than expected
- the matrix clarifies thinking but does not replace thinking

## Style guidance

The thinking should be:
- sharp
- specific
- structured
- non-generic
- intellectually honest

The writing should avoid:
- vague business language
- fake precision
- defaulting to strategy-speak
- pretending the matrix settles everything

The goal is not to create a neat diagram, but a reality more legible through strong axis design.
